> “Where is everybody?” 

<span class="dropcap">T</span>his question was offered by physicist Enrico Fermi and has been pondered upon by thousands and thousands of people since before Fermi existed and also after Fermi died. Are we alone in the universe? Surely there would be sentient life out there among billions and billions of stars?

The stars have always been our oldest companions scattered across the night sky since before the dawn of Earth. We have started to reach out to them, charting the night sky, observing faraway objects, sending out and detecting all sorts of electromagnetism, and we haven’t heard back at all. This contradiction between expectation and observation is what we now call the Fermi Paradox. 
By the mathematical odds, the scale of the cosmos, the billion-year head start other star systems which were formed before the Sun may have had, life should be abundant. Intelligent life ought to be visible. So, where is everyone? All the evidence and deductions point towards an answer which refuses to reveal itself.
Theories regarding this paradox range from philosophical to economic to sociological to psychological. Some propose that intelligent life inevitably destroys itself through war, resource exhaustion, or technologies beyond its own wisdom. 
Some suggest that civilizations retreat into simulations, or evolve beyond physical form, becoming silent witnesses to the universe they no longer wish to shape. Some simply say that we just don’t have the ability to communicate with other intelligent life. 
There are some wilder theories, too- that we live in a simulation, that we are a zoo and being subjected to deliberate isolation; Or that we are being ignored, for reasons that lie beyond our comprehension.
But there is another possibility that life itself is rare, not in probability, but in patience. That distances are too vast, timescales too broad. 
That the universe is speaking; just not in a language we yet understand. Maybe assuming that communication through our methods must be obvious is wrong. Or perhaps the paradox is not cosmic at all, perhaps it is a product of human psychology. 
The desire for contact may be non-existent for the universe, while for us it is large.
By asking, “Where is everybody?” we question our expectations more than we question reality. The stars are still out there, indifferent to the workings of a tiny planet orbiting a tiny sun in a tiny galaxy. Whether they hold watchers or not is, in a way, irrelevant. 
And maybe that’s acceptable, maybe by the very act of wondering, listening hopefully is what places us in conversation with the universe even if it never answers.
